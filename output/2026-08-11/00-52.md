## We are chasing Ebola virus - it is ahead of us, WHO warns
![Image 1: Reuters A health worker in green scrubs helps another person put on their PPE. The person on the left of the picture is wearing full white protective gear and goggles.](https://ichef.bbci.co.uk/news/480/cpsprodpb/d4fc/live/e95d8e40-94da-11f1-8a7b-e7300179227e.jpg.webp)Reuters
Health workers need to be fully protected as the virus can spread from touching bodily fluids.
The latest Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo began at least three months before it was officially declared - meaning the health authorities are still playing catch-up, the World Health Organization (WHO) has warned.
More than 1,900 people have died from the virus since that declaration on 15 May, making this the second-worst Ebola outbreak ever.
Infection rates are still high, adding to the concern that the authorities are struggling to get to grips with the spread of the disease.
The epicentre of this outbreak of the Bundibugyo species of Ebola, is in the east of DR Congo where insecurity has hampered control efforts.
There is also no approved vaccine or any recognised therapeutic drugs, making treating Bundibugyo harder.
"We are chasing the virus, the virus is ahead of us," WHO Africa director Dr Mohamed Janabi said at a news conference in the city of Bunia, which is at the heart of this outbreak.
He cited research showing that the virus started spreading in February and was initially misdiagnosed as malaria or typhoid.
Janabi also said that, in part because of insecurity, health workers are only reaching about 30% of the cases with the rest dying at home.
Traditional burial practices – where mourners touch the deceased's body - and a suspicion of health authorities in some communities have also made things difficult.
But there are now several dedicated treatment centres in the east of DR Congo and people are getting diagnosed much more quickly.
So far there have been 4,294 confirmed cases and 1,960 deaths, DR Congo's health ministry has said.
The largest recorded outbreak was in West Africa between 2014 and 2016 when 11,325 people died.

Warm weather usually drives salad sales, but consumers spooked by the ongoing [cyclospora outbreaks](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/27/cyclospora-outbreak-food-industry-consolidation.html) are avoiding lettuce this summer.
Grocery stores aren’t immune either. Dollar sales of prepackaged salads plunged 14% during the four weeks ended July 25 compared with the year-ago period, according to NielsenIQ data.
The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/outbreaks-foodborne-illness/investigation-15-state-outbreak-cyclospora-illnesses-iceberg-lettuce-july-2026) has pointed to iceberg lettuce processed in Taylor Farms’ central Mexico facility as the likely culprit for the outbreak that has sickened at least 10,000 people. Taylor Farms has voluntarily recalled products supplied from that facility.
[Yum Brands’](https://www.cnbc.com/quotes/YUM/) Taco Bell is the only national restaurant chain that has been linked to the multistate outbreak. It uses iceberg lettuce frequently across its menu, from its Crunchwrap Supreme to its Cheesy Gordita Crunches, but the chain isn’t known for its salads. Taco Bell’s sales and traffic to its restaurants initially tumbled after the FDA announcement, but [Yum executives said](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/30/yum-brands-yum-q2-2026-earnings.html) in late July that business was already recovering.
But the FDA is also tracking at least [six other active outbreaks](https://www.fda.gov/food/outbreaks-foodborne-illness/investigations-foodborne-illness-outbreaks) without a clear culprit; those outbreaks have significantly smaller number of reported cases. The long incubation period for cyclosporiasis makes it difficult to identify the contaminated ingredients.
Cyclospora is a water-borne parasite. It typically spreads through contaminated produce, like lettuce, green onions, raspberries and fresh herbs. Although public health authorities seem to have pinpointed the source of the current outbreak, the FDA is advising consumers to take extra steps, like discarding outer layers of fruits and vegetables, to reduce risk of exposure.
