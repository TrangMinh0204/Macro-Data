## Researchers tracked a long-legged bird’s migration. He flew 6,800 miles.
![Image 2](https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https%3A%2F%2Fcloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com%2Fwapo%2FZY6WFNC4H2BQPMC4C4WW5WRIKA.png&w=992)
A map shows Phil’s journey from the east coast of England to the Taymyr Peninsula in central Siberia on a southern route, and his return via a northern route. (Katharine Bowgen / British Trust for Ornithology)
The bar-tailed godwit is a [long-billed, long-legged shorebird](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Bar-tailed_Godwit/overview "https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Bar-tailed_Godwit/overview") species with some of the longest migration routes in the world — a juvenile named B6 set a record when it traveled [8,425 miles nonstop from Alaska to Australia’s Tasmania](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/alaska-science-center/news/juvenile-bar-tailed-godwit-b6-sets-world-record "https://www.usgs.gov/centers/alaska-science-center/news/juvenile-bar-tailed-godwit-b6-sets-world-record") over 11 days in 2022.
Now, researchers in eastern England have mapped the species’ British migration route for the first time, using a six-gram solar-powered GPS tracker to follow a bird they named Phil.
Researchers described Phil’s “epic” journey [in a statement this week](https://www.rspb.org.uk/whats-happening/news/bar-tailed-godwit-phil-returns-to-englands-east-coast "https://www.rspb.org.uk/whats-happening/news/bar-tailed-godwit-phil-returns-to-englands-east-coast"), saying they tracked him journeying from the east coast of England to the Taymyr Peninsula in central Siberia.
On his way back, the researchers said, he stopped by northern Norway, before returning to England.
The entire trip took 203 days and covered 6,800 miles. By contrast, a flight from London to Los Angeles is about 5,500 miles.
![Image 3](https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https%3A%2F%2Fcloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com%2Fwapo%2FBTWDBW3J3YMODXN3LGAICGQXCE.jpg&w=1440&impolicy=high_res)
A GPS tag is fitted onto a bar-tailed godwit in Lincolnshire, England, in 2023. (Ben Andrew/RSPB)
While Phil’s journey wasn’t record-breaking, conservationists are still celebrating his journey as an “incredible feat” and say the study has revealed new information about bird migration, including the fact that the species bred in Siberia.
“Phil shows that we still have a lot to learn about [bird] migrations and the places that they need to stop to refuel,” ornithologist Nigel Clark of the Wash Wader Research Group told The Washington Post.
